# Hydrogen At Scale 

Hydrogen remains one of the most exciting applications of clean , renewable energy. Its production has been demonstrated  on an industrial scale. Today the price of hydrogen is at an all time low of about $5/kg. That's a kilogram of fuel for about the price of a cold brew coffee. Yet green hydrogen has not been as successful as other fuels or alternative clean energy technologies like batteries. 

This was among the main points of discussion at the conferences I attended in New Delhi and those organised online by Society for Automotive Engineers (SAE) in the USA. One of the most important reasons for slow adoption of green hydrogen is simply the fierce competition it faces from Non Renewable fuels that are so cheap that it makes even a small price of $5/Kg look expensive. 

One solution was proposed during the presentation of GenH2 where they suggested using decommissioned oil wells to produce H2 in situ using gasification (a combination of water gas reforming and steam reforming) making hydrogen as cheap as $1/kg. 

But even with these solutions there are a few technical challenges. One is the inherent low volumetric energy density of hydrogen leaving a few options to make it viable as a fuel. One is liquefaction. It works but requires extremely specialised insulation to keep H2 liquified at -259 C. Hitting that low temp is not easy — either during the liquefaction process or during its use. It works where the fuel is used continuously like in aviation or in shipment but it's a fight against heat. At these low temps even the ambient black body radiation is enough to vaporise the gas. 

The other alternative is more practical where h2 is compressed to ~70Mpa. There are no “boil off” issues and compressed h2 can remain stored for many days. But such high pressures require extremely sturdy cylinders, often composites and powerful compression equipment. 

We can see that simply producing hydrogen is not enough. To make it useful it needs supporting infrastructure that is technically very demanding. 

Presentation by South West Research Institute showcased their work on adapting a diesel Internal Combustion Engine for hydrogen combustion demonstrating that existing technology could work — though it's likely that some specialised coating for continued high temp use will have to be developed. Despite that, this is definitely a strong demonstration in favor of H2 adoption. 

Assuming an abundant renewable electricity supply, H2 electrolysers rely on small gaps to minimize resistive losses and an expensive material stack to make it happen. Electrodes are made up of Iridium,Platinum,Ni, Molybdenum and other alloys. Specialised membranes are built with technical polymers that require extreme precision, leaving absolutely no room for error. Modern electrolyser stack is reliant on these membranes because electrodes are tightly spaced ,due to the need to minimise resistance , and hence membranes are essential to keep the generated gasses separate. 

A presentation by the University of Cincinnati addressed the issue of energy and water. For entire transportation needs of USA an area the size of Connecticut could provide enough power to generate all the hydrogen needed. On the other hand the freshwater requirement would only be .08% of the total water usage. 

These numbers prove that desalination is not a strict requirement for the hydrogen economy. But the truth is that not every region has equal access to water. Some are severely resource starved and desal essentially removes all constraints on water while giving access to 2 more industrially important chemicals NaOH and HCl. So many policy makers are actively investigating sea water as a platform for building 3 industrially important chemical streams. Not to mention the potential of geoengineering if we are able to desalinate on a large scale.Think more secure food supply and carbon fixation in woody biomass — all of this with abundant water supply. 

On paper the prospects of H2 look very good. There are genuine reasons to be excited about its future. But small bottlenecks like reliance on rare materials or complex manufacturing techniques could become a problem when production is scaled to billions of tonnes. Ni based AEM stacks are built on more abundant materials but Ni is not exactly evenly distributed. It's geographically concentrated in a few spots. 

Imagine having to replace electrodes and membranes every 10 years or so. This essentially recreates the global electrolyser infrastructure every decade. Is that possible? Anything is possible. The ingenuity that engineers and scientists have shown so far is spectacular. But it wouldn't hurt to continue to search for cheaper, more straightforward solutions that are fault tolerant. That way real industrial decentralisation would be possible and lead to a far greater opportunity for wealth creation. 

The same goes for hydrogen as a fuel. Yes it's possible via liquefaction or compression but there are also developments in the area of Sustainable aviation fuels that builds kerosene like molecules from captured carbon and green hydrogen. This makes kerosene fully green and something that is instantly compatible with the technological stack that we have today. While with fuels we have several options - H2 NH3, Ch4 and so on , hydrocarbons remain indispensable for polymers. To be fully renewable it is important to look at both energy and materials renewability. 

This has been an incredible year for me when it comes to interacting with engineers and scientists working on hydrogen technologies. I look forward to continuing this engagement in the future and witnessing hydrogen science pushing boundaries of what's possible.
