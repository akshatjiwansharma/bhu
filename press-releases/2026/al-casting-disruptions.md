# From castings to composites — can Al manufacturers help disrupt the carbon industry? 

Aluminium is one of the most important industrial materials renowned for being as light as ceramics despite having a metallic character. It possesses high thermal conductivity,excellent reflectivity and electrical conductivity that is higher than steel making it a direct competitor to iron and its alloy systems. 

Globally the Al market is valued at ~$180B with 72 Million Tonnes of Al produced annually. India is the world's second largest producer at 4.2 M tonnes of annual production and $12 B of market value. Aluminium being important both technologically and economically I was glad to attend the conference organised by Aluminium casters association of India and learn about what new technical and business innovations are being planned for this metal. 

I was excited to learn that the industry leaders have recognised applications beyond the automotive and packaging sector. Because of its high electrical conductivity one of the most lucrative non automotive applications for Al is as electrical wires. Al conductors steel reinforced (ACSR) and Al conductors composite core (ACCC) have already demonstrated their usefulness in high voltage grid lines. 

Given how electrification is prioritised by governments around the world, demand for Al as a cheaper alternative to copper is expected to rise. 

Besides that Al remains important in other highly technical sectors for example semiconductors in silicon metallisation, aluminium gallium arsenide LEDs, aluminium nitride substrates and others. 

For Alucast one of the main focus areas was on die casting of Al and the members here identified semi solid casting as a promising new direction for the industry that is heavily reliant on High Pressure liquid metal die casting at present. 

Also identified were some bottlenecks in raw Al metal manufacturing. Despite being a light weight material Al takes 40-80 kwh/kg of embodied energy to manufacture using current techniques. Losses happen at furnace level, during electrode consumption due to O2 evolution and electrical energy generation step. 

While Al metal has a low melting point of only 600C it is produced from Al2O3 which melts at temperatures exceeding 2000 C. While industry already manufactures Silicon at extremely high temperatures and methods also exist to fuse Al2O3 to make synthetic ruby at 2000 C, for mass production of Al fluxing agents are used that drop the temp required to around 1100C.

With  optimised setups and fully electric furnaces raw Al metal production could be achieved at about 20-30Kwh/kg levels. This would make the industry more efficient. Even as Al itself remains a highly recyclable material. 

However the main reason that makes me really excited about Al is that despite being relatively expensive to produce from an energetic point of view nearly 1.6 Billion tonnes of Al have been produced globally. This is significant because the operating temperature of furnaces and properties of Al namely high strength and conductivity matches with another potentially disruptive material class of Carbon materials. 

Optimised setups of carbon furnaces can produce it at <20 kwh/kg and they are just as light as Al—slightly lighter in fact. They possess anisotropic electrical and thermal conductivities that may prove to be impractical at low voltages but become very appealing in HV setups. Both classes of materials retain their strength upto 300 C but carbon does start to oxidise while Al remains stable. 

The advantage that Carbon offers is unprecedented abundance. Newer methods like molten salt synthesis allow use of temperatures in the range of 700-900c to perform the carbonisation step falling within the range of what Al manufacturers are already used to. Additionally carbon composites both cc and CFRP can be prepared using injection molding techniques followed by densification process. 

I'm not the only one who is thinking along these lines. Rockman group ,one of the sponsors of this event, already has a cfrp manufacturing unit. While it remains focussed on Al materials, the carbon materials ecosystem is rapidly maturing and into something that has the potential to disrupt this low weight high strength functional/structural materials space.  


It is important that industry leaders continue to invest in research and development of new technologies and also find ways in which they can apply their existing skills and tools to create something new. 

As a non profit organisation Alucast has done an incredible job in bringing together industry and academia and creating interest in materials engineering. While Al will continue to remain the focus of the group, bringing together people who work on different aspects of manufacturing and even on different materials systems will only help the overall materials science industry.  I look forward to continuing my engagement with Alucast.
